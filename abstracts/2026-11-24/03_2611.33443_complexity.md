# Extrinsic thermodynamic geometry, symmetry, and the cost of constraint

- **arXiv**: [2611.33443](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33443)
- **Date**: 2026-11-24
- **Category**: complexity
- **Authors**: Jordan R. Sawchuk, David A. Sivak
- **NOFX Relevance Score**: 3

## Abstract

Driving a stochastic system in finite time costs excess work; exactly how much depends on which system parameters, and hence which paths in distribution space, a controller can access. In the friction-tensor formalism for slow driving, the space of equilibrium distributions is a Riemannian manifold and constraints on a controller define submanifolds. Whether a constrained controller is forced onto suboptimal paths is a question of the extrinsic thermodynamic geometry of the submanifold, which we develop for discrete systems. We show that submanifolds with zero extrinsic curvature can be identified from symmetries of the transition network, before any optimization. A minimum-work protocol on such a submanifold is therefore stationary in the excess work even when the constraints are lifted. The stationarity of symmetric optima holds exactly, beyond slow driving. In the slow-driving regime, we show that the ambient curvature transverse to a symmetric submanifold determines whether nearby symmetry-broken protocols have lower excess work. This curvature, which separates into independent contributions from each pattern of symmetry breaking, thus acquires an operational meaning as a measure of the thermodynamic incentive to break a given symmetry. A thermodynamic comparison theorem provides an inexpensive test of local minimality that weighs the extremal curvatures along the symmetric protocol against its excess work. There is no need for such tests under certain convexity conditions on the transition rates and control parameters, which guarantee that a symmetric optimum is a global optimum. These results certify the global optimality of a five-parameter protocol previously observed to match 15-parameter full control of a four-spin Ising system, and explain why partial symmetry breaking is optimal for the same system under local-field control.
