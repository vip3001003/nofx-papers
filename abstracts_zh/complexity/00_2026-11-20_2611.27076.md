# Quantum order by disorder among lozenge tilings: a staircase of hub-free crystals between the Lieb ferrimagnet and the antiferromagnet

- **arXiv**: [2611.27076](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27076)
- **日期**: 2026-11-20
- **分类**: complexity
- **作者**: Suhwan Yoo, Jeehoon Choi, Chaeyeon Kim et al.
- **NOFX相关度**: 0

## 摘要

Lozenge tilings of the triangular lattice define an exponentially large family of bipartite networks, from the dice lattice, a Lieb ferrimagnet with the largest sublattice imbalance, to square-type tilings, which are compensated antiferromagnets. For the classical Heisenberg antiferromagnet all of them are degenerate. We show that quantum fluctuations lift this degeneracy through a local mechanism: the zero-point energy is, to leading order, a sum of site terms that are convex in the coordination $z$, so that Jensen's inequality at fixed $\langle z\rangle=4$ selects the square-type tiling. An expansion about the Ising limit explains the convexity: a site with more bonds has more channels for quantum fluctuations, but each fluctuation frustrates more bonds. A magnetic field couples to the Lieb moment and competes with this selection. Because the moment is carried most cheaply by threefold and fivefold sites, sixfold hubs are avoided, and bond counting together with the Lieb--Mattis theorem limits hub-free tilings to $3/5$ of the dice moment. Quantum Monte Carlo and a complete enumeration of periodic tilings with small unit cells show that the classical jump is replaced by a staircase of periodic crystals. Its widest step is a hub-free crystal that saturates the bound; below it, threefold and fivefold defects pack ever more densely in a shallow sequence of crystals, controlled by a short-ranged attraction between defects of equal curvature and a repulsion between defects of opposite curvature. Selection energies scale as $JS$. In the height representation the coordination is the discrete Gaussian curvature and the Lieb moment its sublattice-staggered part. We give spectroscopic signatures and discuss realizations in nanographenes, atomic spin arrays and quantum simulators.
